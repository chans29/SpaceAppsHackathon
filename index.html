Zero-Gravity Exercise

Without gravity, bones and muscles weaken. Astronauts on the International Space Station (ISS) work out about two hours a day on machines built for weightlessness. [1][2]

How astronauts train

  • Plan on about two hours of exercise a day, mixing strength training and cardio. [1][2]
  • Strap in first. The machines use harnesses, clip-in shoes and hand grips so you don't float away mid-workout. [2]
  • Short, hard workouts work too. NASA found that shorter, higher-intensity sessions gave results similar to longer ones. [1]
  • Results vary from person to person, so routines are set by an exercise specialist. [1][2]

Squats (ARED machine)

The ARED (Advanced Resistive Exercise Device) uses vacuum cylinders to create up to 272 kg (600 lb) of resistance, like lifting free weights. Set the resistance, position the bar on your shoulders and squat. [2]

Deadlifts (ARED machine)

Use the same ARED bar to lift from low to standing. This works the legs, hips and back, which helps keep bones strong. [2]

Heel raises (ARED machine)

With the bar loaded, rise up onto your toes and lower back down. This works the calves, which barely get used when you're floating. [2]

Running (treadmill with harness)

Put on the harness, which pulls your shoulders and hips down toward the belt. The pull starts at about 60% of your body weight and is raised to about 85% over the mission. Then run. [2]

Cycling (CEVIS bike)

The space bike has no seat. Clip your cycling shoes into the pedals, hold the hand grips to stay in place, and pedal. A computer keeps the resistance steady. [1][2]

Sources

  1. NASA, “Astronaut Exercise,” nasa.gov
  2. Canadian Space Agency, “Physical activity in space,” asc-csa.gc.ca

CPR in Zero Gravity

On Earth, gravity holds the patient down and lets you use your body weight. In weightlessness, pushing on someone's chest just pushes you both apart, so the rescuer has to anchor themselves first. [1][2]

Before you start

  • On Earth? Call 911 and do standard CPR.
  • Alert the rest of the crew and the flight surgeons (space doctors) on the ground right away. [5]
  • Get the defibrillator (AED), which shocks the heart back into rhythm, from the medical kit. [6]
  • If you can, strap the patient to the folding patient restraint table so they don't drift away. [6]

Compression targets

  • Push about 5 to 6 cm (2 to 2.4 inches) deep. [1]
  • Aim for 100 to 120 compressions per minute. [3]

Handstand method (NASA's current method)

Float above the patient. Brace your legs against the wall or ceiling of the spacecraft, then push down on their chest with straight arms, like doing a handstand on it. Use this when there's a surface to push your feet against. [1]

Evetts-Russomano method (open space)

Use this when there's no wall close enough to brace against. Wrap your left leg over the patient's right shoulder and your right leg around their waist, and cross your ankles behind their back. That locks you to their body so you can do compressions. [4]

Reverse bear hug

A third hands-only method, done from behind the patient. In testing, it and the Evetts-Russomano method did not push deep enough. [1]

Machine CPR (newer research)

A 2025 study in simulated weightlessness found that a mechanical piston device reached the target depth. The handstand method averaged about 3.5 cm, which is too shallow. Researchers hope future space guidelines include the device. [1]

This page is a quick reference, not training. Take a certified CPR course to learn hands-on.

First Aid in Zero Gravity

Every astronaut gets medical training and stays in touch with doctors on the ground. [1] The basics of first aid are the same as on Earth, but floating changes how you do them.

First steps

  • Anchor yourself and the patient first. The station has a folding patient restraint table that also works as a stretcher. [3]
  • Contact the flight surgeons (space doctors) on the ground. They can talk you through treatment. [1][3]
  • Treat the biggest threat first: make sure the person can breathe, then stop any bleeding. [4]

Bleeding

Blood doesn't drip in weightlessness. It bubbles up into a dome over the wound, and drops can break off and float around the cabin. [3] Press firmly and keep pressing with a bandage or cloth, and pack deep wounds with gauze. [5] For life-threatening bleeding, use a tourniquet and clotting gauze early. [4]

Protect faces from floating blood

Floating drops of blood can drift into the patient's eyes, nose and throat, so keep the wound covered while you work. [6]

What's in the medical kit

The station has a first aid kit, a pharmacy (medicines), a defibrillator, a portable ultrasound and devices to help with breathing. [1][2][3]

Astronaut medical training

Before flying, astronauts take about 40 hours of medical training. They learn skills like stitching a wound, pulling a tooth, giving a shot and reading an ultrasound. [2]

Serious injuries

If someone needs urgent care the station can't give, the crew returns to Earth in the spacecraft they launched on. [1]

This page is a quick reference, not training. On Earth, call 911 for emergencies and take a certified first aid course.
